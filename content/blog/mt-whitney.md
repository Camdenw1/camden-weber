I was prepared. I was confident. I was aware of the risks. It was hard. It was completed. Now it’s legacy.

Last Thanksgiving I went to Sequoia National Park with my family and Nathan. On top of Moro Rock, I looked over and saw a great collection of mountains. I learned that Mount Whitney was part of that collection.

Mount Whitney is the tallest mountain in the US excluding Alaska. You’ll often hear people call it the lower 48 or the contiguous 48. There is something special about it.

I started looking into what it takes to summit. You need a permit which you score from a lottery that opens in February. If you want to have the safest and easiest weather conditions you will want to go around August. It is a 22-mile hike straight up and down a mountain.

22 miles didn’t scare me. I’ve run 26.2 miles. Why would *walking* 22 miles scare me? I found out the hard way…

You may think I didn’t train correctly, but I honestly think I was in good shape to do this hike. I wasn’t really sore the next day and was even sprinting around running football routes. So let me run you through how I “trained.”

I decided I would take an approach by simply staying active in whatever way I found fun while doing maybe 1 exercise a week where I was specifically thinking about how it would benefit my Whitney training.

Basketball, running, and hiking were my big three activities. I was in an adult league with my friends so I played at least once a week no matter what. On top of that, I signed up for three San Diego half marathons, the same three my parents ran in a single year years back. This made exercise meaningful and easy. I also planned many fun hike days with my friends where we would aim to hike one of the peaks in San Diego.

We also did a first-ever group backpacking trip in Catalina where we did some serious miles and elevation gain. The 2 days combined were about equivalent to Whitney in miles and total gain. This surged my confidence. I knew that we weren’t at altitude but thought the added difficulty of having a heavy pack would cancel out.

The group started with four people but dwindled to just two: Jake G. and me. He is a dog man. I had faith in this guy after his performance in Catalina. Never once doubted this man’s hiking ability and he proved me right.

Jake and I got all the gear, food, maps, and plan sorted out and started our drive up. We were planning on having altitude medicine but ended up not getting it. Going into it, I had two main concerns.

The first concern is altitude. I have been up to 12k before with only a very minor headache, but never 14.5k. Since we were starting from about 8k, this meant more elevation gain and a higher altitude than I’d ever experienced.

The second concern is ensuring my muscles don’t cramp and don’t give out. During my marathons and half marathons, I push myself to a point where my muscles cramp right at the end. Near the end of these races, it feels like I can’t run any farther even if I want to. I was scared this could happen on Whitney because I had never done a hike of this level.

We woke up around 12 and started hiking by 1 AM. It seems early but many people start that early. Hiking in the dark in such a majestic place is something I will never forget. The views when the sun was rising marked the end of bliss and the start of despair despite how pretty it was.

I slowly started feeling the effects of altitude. From 12k-13.5k it felt like I was just ridiculously tired. I was aware that what I was feeling could be altitude sickness but I only got about 2 hours of sleep the night before and just hiked almost 10 miles straight uphill, so I thought it very plausible it was just normal fatigue.

I started getting to a point where I felt so tired that I took a nap on the side of the trail. This actually made me feel so much better, but only for the next 45 minutes. We had less than 2 miles left and that sounded very doable to me. These 2 miles were the hardest 2 miles of my whole life. I believe the 2 miles plus the break we took at the top totaled about 2 hours.

I started feeling straight up sick. All I wanted to do is be back home in my bed doing nothing but recover. I was so close to the top though. I could literally see the top and it was a healthy Camden’s 15 minutes away.

I felt in control overall and powered through. Looking back this was probably a stupid decision considering my state. Morale was low on this last climb. I would like to shout out Jake for being the man in this situation. He said he wasn’t going to leave me and encouraged going down if I needed. He never once complained and was very supportive the whole time. Couldn’t have done it without him.

Getting to the top was an accomplishment for sure. I knew the descent would be much better. We still had 11+ miles of hiking to go and I was not feeling good.

This was where the physical battle started shifting into a mental battle. How long can you endure? There is quite literally no easy way out. We talked to random people on the way down and also just cruised eating miles as fast as we could.

After 4ish hours, we made it back to the car. I have never been so happy to see my old little Mazda 3 in my life. Water, snacks, change of clothes, air conditioning, comfy seats, and more made me appreciate the comforts of my car more than I ever have in my life. Also, putting your feet in a cold stream after a long hike is one of the best feelings ever. Highly recommend.

Jake was a beast and was able to drive us home after. Life didn’t feel real that night. Hardest day of my life and one I will remember forever.

When you do something hard, you learn. Your perception of the world changes. Your confidence in yourself changes. Your relationships change. Experiencing this long, iconic day hike allowed me to relate to those who have done Whitney before and other big climbs. I have a newfound respect for these people as now I know what they went through.

Additionally, you gain respect for the environment. Nature is strong and you have to use the best of your human abilities if you plan on spending an extended period of time in nature. You get in touch with your basic needs. You also appreciate the skills that humans have that have led us to be such a thriving species. When in nature you relate to other forms of life. You see more life around water and in sheltered areas from the gusting winds and the drying sun. You appreciate the protection from the elements that trees and mountains provide.

Most of all, I find the trail one of the easiest places to connect with other humans. I’ve met the nicest and most positive people on the trail while also having some of my favorite moments with my friends on the trail. It creates something special. In a day and age where people are more and more sucked into the addictive and socially isolating technologies coupled with the comforts of modern homes, the trail is one of the only ways to feel like you are fully living life the way it was meant to be lived.
