## Creation

It was an ordinary night. I just got back from a friend's birthday party and got back to researching possible outdoors trips to put on the calendar. I have accumulated such a desire to get out of the San Diego desert and be out in new nature. Get both a literal and figurative breath of fresh air.

I often look at permits to gauge how far in advance you have to plan for certain trips. I noticed that the Rae Lakes Loop trailhead, Bubb's Creek, had a single open permit for a Saturday in a couple weeks. I knew that this wasn't going to be available for long, so I pounced on the opportunity. The scarcity of supply is what pushed me to obtain the permit. Now, I was faced with quite the adventure in front of me if I go through with this.

Logistically, this is a 7-hour drive to a 42-mile hike with 8,200 feet of total elevation gain followed by a 7-hour drive back. Additionally, this will be my first time in the backcountry alone, with no cell service. The isolation and disconnection was daunting for sure. However, the uniqueness of this adventure attracted me a great deal.

I have never had a plan continuously intrigue and excite me as much as this plan. It would fully satisfy my craving to get outdoors somewhere new. The possible wildlife there, bears, fish, deer, and more, had me itching to roll the dice and see if I could see some with my own eyes.

There were some obstacles too: mosquitoes, a snowy pass, and a harsh river crossing. These didn't dissuade me but actually encouraged me to go. I wanted to experience a challenge. I wanted to be pushed slightly out of my comfort zone. If I wasn't, I would feel as though this wasn't worth it. Once I realized that I actually might go, I started talking through my plan with friends and family. At this point, I had fully thought through the logistics. I planned out my drives, chunked the hike up in sections, planned my camping locations, and also ensured I was fully prepared to ensure I was safe. This meant planning out food, route finding, and many other small things. Talking through my plan with people was great because they were able to foolproof my plan and ensure I was fully ready. I saved my main backpacking buddies for a last check and that proved to be worth it as it cemented my confidence.

It was time for me to depart the real world and embark on this adventure!

## The Adventure

I came into this with three things I wanted to accomplish. The biggest was seeing a bear with my own two eyes, followed by catching a fish all by myself, and finally getting to the top of Glen Pass and taking in the view. The real overall goal of this whole adventure was to get closer to myself though. Prove to myself that I can do something hard, alone. Prove to myself that I love myself enough to spend 3 whole days with just myself and my own thoughts.

The first part of the adventure was me stopping at Bass Pro Shops and REI. Here I bought a tenkara rod, a simple Japanese rod that gives an extremely simple fishing experience. It is a super small and light rod that telescopes out and can be packed to catching fish within a minute. I also bought a button down hiking/fishing shirt and toe socks. Amassing some gear for each trip that I go on has been one of my favorite activities.

The next part of the adventure was camping at the trailhead alone. I got camp set up and then decided to walk around. Here, I saw a bear! I felt like this didn't count because I was not in the wilderness but nonetheless. Additionally, I ran into a ranger presentation about the impact of photography and how it was a driving force to spread awareness of beautiful landscapes that should be preserved as pure nature. I thought this was extremely interesting and it also allowed me to converse with many people who shared the same passion for nature.

The next morning, I got up bright and early to go pick up my permit where I met people from all places and backgrounds. One guy I met was planning on doing 60 miles over 2 days. Absolutely insane. These people ended up being people I would run into over the trip.

The hike started with stunning mountain views from the canyon floor which put a smile on my face. About an hour into the hike, I rounded the corner and saw a bear eating berries. How surreal it was to be alone in the wilderness with a bear munching on some plants. This was the moment that I already knew the whole adventure was worth it.

After that, the Bubb's Creek switchbacks came up. I found it hilarious that they had a name because that must mean it's quite the climb… and that it was. I enjoyed it though. I always find climbing mountains to be fun because you get up in elevation so quick and continuously get improved views.

This trail took you along the rising canyon floor, along a roaring river, with many plants, trees, and mountains to appease the eyes. I stopped for some food; it was a block of cheese. While I was eating this, the absolute beast of a man who was hiking 60 miles in 2 days passed me. It was great talking to him and getting to know how he got to that point, but man having someone walk in on you eating a straight block of cheese is pretty funny.

Later on, I stopped by the river and just took some time to recharge and enjoy the beauty of the river. When you backpack it feels like every time you eat you are doing your back a service by decreasing the weight.

Throughout my hike I had many conversations, stopping and talking with just about everyone who passed. I met PCT hikers, JMT hikers, and fellow Rae Lakes Loop hikers. The level of these through hikers was far higher than anyone I have come into contact with in the past so I used it to ask them questions. From gear improvement advice to advice on how to fit big backpacking trips into regular life, I got countless amazing tips and more importantly, genuine human connection. These interactions were some of the most positive and real interactions I've ever had. These people have been so focused on the most basic necessities in life for the past month or so. I believe that contributed to just how real and authentic these people are.

Apart from human connection, I also went through some fish connection as well through the trip. I was consistently eyeing the water trying to spot fish to see if there was good fishing. The first fish I saw and consequently tried to fish was at a shallow pool on top of a waterfall. A stunning spot. This fish ultimately got away but hope was sparked. Later on, I fished at my first campsite. Here I got a bite, but more importantly, I decided I wanted to make some changes to my fishing setup.

I printed out a page with knot tutorials. I used this to cut down the total amount of line on my rod to give me more control. This was an awesome upgrade that made a simple rod even simpler and easier to use. Later on, I used my newly adjusted rod to fish in a lake at 11,000 feet. While I didn't catch anything, I got much more efficient at unpacking and packing my rod.

I knew there were many lakes on the other side of obstacle number one, Glen Pass. Switchbacks and steep climbs at altitude proved to be a slog. The first 5 miles that day felt like they were the longest 5 miles I have ever hiked. Although, getting to the top was one of the best feelings ever. The panoramic view of lakes and mountains in the great Sierra Nevada was unreal. The descent down to the lakes felt like the drive back home after a long day of work with a delicious home cooked meal waiting for you. I've never experienced that, but I imagine they are similar feelings. As I descended, more and more greenery appeared. The Rae Lakes were beautifully blue, surrounded by towering gray mountains, and a perfect amount of bushes, trees, flowers, and grass creating a nature utopia. Never have I ever seen somewhere so elegant and peaceful.

I stopped for lunch and while I was eating, I noticed 1 fish, 2 fish, re… 3 fish, 4 fish. I knew this was it. I took out my rod and within a couple minutes caught my first ever fish fully on my own. I did this 5 more times before stopping to continue on with my lunch and hike. This section of the hike was by far my favorite. The views were unreal and the people were happy. My only problem with this place was that I wasn't able to stay there long enough. It was truly special.

The end of this day, day 2, was essentially a 10 mile race to camp against the sun. I joke with my friends about hikes having hidden miles because for some reason 3 miles on trail almost always feels far longer than 3 miles through a city. I knew what campsite I wanted to get to and I wasn't going to quit. I knew that worst case, I could hike with my headlamp. After 3-4 hours of head-down trudging, I finally made it to camp… almost. There was a dangerous river crossing separating me and camp. The sun had set and I knew my clothes would be wet through the night. I found the widest section of the river and used my poles to help myself keep balance through the strong stream.

I arrived at my final camp quite wet. Luckily, I was so tired and hungry that being cold was low on my worries list. Additionally, I made a fire which helped warm me up and dry some of my clothes. I went to bed this night with the finish line in mind and a sense of accomplishment. I had cleared all the obstacles and finished all my goals. Tomorrow was merely 10 miles of downhill hiking and a long drive back.

Tomorrow went according to plan. My friend Fossa hosted me for dinner and I got home late that night. I felt more accomplished than I had before and I felt equipped with some great stories to share. It was awesome seeing all my friends and family, especially the ones I briefed.

I felt more connected with myself, humanity, nature, and the stuff that is important in life was more clear. Backpacking is an exercise in mental, physical, and spiritual health and I would recommend it to everyone.
